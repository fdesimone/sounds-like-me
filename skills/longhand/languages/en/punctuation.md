# English punctuation

Essential rules to copy into the personal skill, together with the preferences the person states. English has two main conventions, American and British. Do not pick one yourself: follow the variety the person writes in and stay consistent. When a personal preference and a rule conflict, the preference wins as long as the text stays correct.

## The marks

- **Comma.** None between subject and verb. A pair around an aside. Before "and", "but", "so" joining two full sentences. Do not join two sentences with a comma alone.
- **Serial comma.** "Red, green, and blue" or "red, green and blue": both are correct. Follow the person's habit.
- **Semicolon.** Between two complete sentences tied by the same thought. Rare in everyday writing: use it only if the person does.
- **Colon.** Before a list, an explanation or a quote. One per sentence. Lowercase after it, unless a proper noun or a quote follows (American usage often capitalizes a full sentence after a colon: follow the person).
- **Full stop.** Ends the sentence. None after headings.
- **Parentheses.** For an addition the reader can skip. The full stop goes after the closing parenthesis unless the parenthesis holds a whole sentence of its own.
- **Quotation marks.** American: double marks, commas and full stops inside. British: often single marks, punctuation outside unless it belongs to the quote. Follow the person.
- **Dashes.** The hyphen joins compound words. The en dash marks ranges. The em dash sets off an aside: correct in English, but see the note in the AI patterns file about overuse. Use it as often as the person does and no more.
- **Question and exclamation marks.** No space before them. Never doubled in work texts.
- **Ellipsis.** Three dots. Sparingly.

## Asides

An aside interrupts the sentence to add or comment, then the sentence goes on. Three forms, from lightest to strongest:

- between commas: "the project, as planned, started in March";
- in parentheses: "the project (the third this year) started in March";
- between dashes, if the person writes them.

Check: remove the aside and the sentence must still stand.

## Apostrophes

- "It's" means "it is". "Its" is the possessive.
- "You're" and "your", "they're" and "their", "who's" and "whose".
- No apostrophe in plain plurals: "the 1990s", "three PDFs".
- Contractions ("don't", "I'll") are part of a person's voice: use them as often as they do.

## Lists

- An introductory line ending in a colon.
- Short items: lowercase, no final punctuation or a semicolon, as the person does.
- Full-sentence items: capital letter and full stop.
- Do not mix the two in one list.

## Numbers, dates, abbreviations

- Full stop for decimals, comma for thousands: 1,250.50.
- Percent sign attached to the number: 42%.
- Dates: "November 15, 2026" (American) or "15 November 2026" (British). Never an all-numeric date for an international reader.
- "e.g." and "i.e." with full stops, followed by a comma in American usage.

## Capitals and spelling

- Headings: sentence case or title case, as the person writes them.
- Spelling follows the person's variety ("color" or "colour", "organize" or "organise") and never mixes.
- In emails, the line after the greeting starts with a capital letter.
