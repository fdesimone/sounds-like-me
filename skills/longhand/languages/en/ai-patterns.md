# AI patterns in English

A starting list of the patterns that make a model-written text recognizable. It builds on [no-ai-slop](https://github.com/petergyang/no-ai-slop) by Peter Yang (MIT license), reorganized to match the other language packs.

How to use it: this is a starting point, not a law. Copy into the personal skill only the entries that matter, and remove the ones the person really uses in texts they wrote by hand. The examples below are invented: replace them with examples from the person's own AI draft when there is one.

## Sentence patterns

- **Two-beat contrast.** "It's not a technology problem. It's a people problem." Also in one sentence: "The question isn't if, it's when." State the second half directly.
- **Negative listing.** "Not a course. Not a manual. A method." Say only the last one.
- **Dramatic fragments.** "That's it. That's the whole thing." Use full sentences.
- **Colon reveal.** "The result: a mess." "The best part: it works." Write a plain sentence. Colons are for lists, explanations and quotes.
- **Self-answered question.** "The reason? Simple." Give the reason.
- **Trailing participle.** A tail that pretends to explain: "highlighting the importance of", "underscoring the role of", "reflecting a broader shift". Replace it with the fact or cut it.
- **Automatic triplets.** Three adjectives or nouns in a row by reflex: "clear, simple and effective". Keep the one that does work.
- **"Not only... but also"** used to inflate a sentence that would stand with one half.
- **"Whether you're... or..."** as an all-purpose opener.
- **Symmetry.** Paragraphs all the same length, sentences all the same shape, balanced pairs one after another.
- **Synonym cycling.** "The company", "the firm", "the organization", "the business" for the same thing in one paragraph. If one word is right, repeat it.

## Openings and closings

- **Throat-clearing.** "It's worth noting that", "It's important to remember", "Here's the thing", "Let me be clear".
- **Fake revelation.** "What nobody tells you", "The part everyone misses", "The uncomfortable truth is".
- **Epochal frame.** "In today's fast-paced world", "In the digital age", "Now more than ever".
- **Announcing the content.** "In this article we'll explore", "Let's dive in", "Let's break it down".
- **Aphorism kicker.** The last line turns the piece into a maxim: "The future isn't waiting. It's being built." Cut it and end on the last concrete fact or the next action.
- **Recap ending.** "In conclusion", "Ultimately", "Overall", followed by a paragraph that repeats the text.
- **Generic invitation.** "What do you think?", "Let me know in the comments" where nobody asked.

## Comments on the text

- **Instructions to the reader.** "This point is crucial", "As we'll see", "This is where it gets interesting", "That last part matters". If the point is clear they are not needed, and if it is not they are not enough.
- **Declared importance.** "Marks a turning point", "plays a key role", "stands as a testament to". State the fact and let the reader judge.
- **Vague attribution.** "Experts agree", "studies show", "it is widely recognized". Name the source and the year, or drop the claim.

## Inflated words

Replace with the plain word unless really needed: delve, leverage, utilize, foster, facilitate, empower, streamline, harness, elevate, embark, unlock, navigate (the complexity), robust, seamless, cutting-edge, groundbreaking, transformative, pivotal, paramount, multifaceted, intricate, tapestry, landscape, realm, journey (figurative), game changer, paradigm shift, ever-evolving.

Adverbs that are usually empty: simply, literally, truly, fundamentally, crucially, inherently, incredibly, significantly, ultimately.

## Assistant formulas in emails

"I hope this email finds you well", "I wanted to reach out", "I'm writing to", "Please don't hesitate to reach out", "Thank you in advance for your time", "Looking forward to hearing from you" used on every message. Keep one only if the person really uses it.

Also the enthusiasm of an assistant answering a prompt: "Great question!", "Absolutely!", "Certainly!", "Here you go!".

## Punctuation tells

- **Em dash everywhere.** People do use the em dash in English, so the mark itself proves nothing. The tell is frequency: several per paragraph, used for every aside and every dramatic pause. Follow the person's own count. If their hand-written texts have none, use commas, parentheses or a full stop.
- **Semicolons in casual messages** from someone who never writes them.
- **Curly quotes and perfect apostrophes** in a chat message from someone whose other messages have straight ones.

## Formatting

- Bold scattered mid-sentence for emphasis.
- Emoji in headings and at the start of lines.
- Bullet lists where two sentences would do.
- A heading over every two-line paragraph.
- Title Case On Every Heading, when the person writes sentence case.
- List items all shaped as "**Label:** explanation".

## Two quick tests

- **The moving test.** If a sentence could move unchanged into another person's text, another company's or another subject's, it is filler. Cut it or replace it with a fact, an example, a consequence.
- **The read-aloud test.** If the sentence sounds like a speech when read to a colleague, rewrite it the way you would say it.
