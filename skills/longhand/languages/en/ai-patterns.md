# AI patterns in English

A starting list of the patterns that make a model-written text recognizable. It builds on [no-ai-slop](https://github.com/petergyang/no-ai-slop) by Peter Yang and on [Humanizer](https://github.com/blader/humanizer) by Siqi Chen (both MIT), which in turn draw on Wikipedia's ["Signs of AI writing"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing), maintained by WikiProject AI Cleanup. Wording and examples here are our own.

## How to read this list

- **It is a starting point, not a law.** Copy into the personal skill only the entries that matter, and remove the ones the person really uses in texts they wrote by hand. Where a person does use a mark or a construction, match how often they use it.
- **Strongest first.** The patterns under "Staging instead of stating" justify a change on one sighting. An entry marked *weak alone* is something careful writers also do on purpose: count it only when other tells sit in the same passage, and never ban it in a personal skill without evidence from the samples.
- **Structure outlasts vocabulary.** Word habits change with every model generation. Sentence and paragraph habits persist, so they come first and the word lists come last.
- The examples are invented. Replace them with examples from the person's own AI draft when there is one.

## Staging instead of stating

The sentence signals importance instead of adding a fact.

- **Two-beat contrast.** "It's not a technology problem. It's a people problem." Also inside one sentence ("The question isn't if, it's when") and as a clipped tail ("It installs in a minute, no setup"). The negative half denies something nobody claimed. State the second half. Keep a contrast only when it corrects a belief the reader really holds.
- **One-line closer.** "That's the real win." "Let that sink in." Also the line after an example that names what the example showed: "The message was clear." Cut it unless it adds a fact.
- **Dramatic fragments.** "No plan. No budget. No time." Merge them into one sentence with a specific claim.
- **Colon reveal.** "The result: a mess." Write a plain sentence. Colons are for lists, explanations and quotes.
- **Self-answered question.** "The reason? Simple." Give the reason.
- **Sayings that sound deep.** "At its core", "The real question is", "Trust is the currency of teams". Replace the saying with the specific claim.
- **Staged run-up.** "Let's dive in", "Here's the thing", "Honestly?" as a standalone opener. Remove the run-up and make the point. The same words inside a casual sentence are ordinary.
- **Arguing with no one.** "To be clear", "I'm not saying", "A tempting approach would be" when no one raised the objection. Remove the defense and keep any real claim inside it.
- **Fake revelation.** "What nobody tells you", "The part everyone misses".
- **Epochal frame.** "In today's fast-paced world", "Now more than ever".
- **Announcing the content.** "In this article we'll explore", "Let's break it down".
- **Aphorism kicker.** The last line turns the piece into a maxim: "The future isn't waiting. It's being built." End on the last concrete fact or the next action.
- **Recap ending.** "In conclusion", "Ultimately", followed by a paragraph that repeats the text.
- **Generic invitation.** "What do you think?", "Let me know in the comments" where nobody asked.

## Rhythm by rule

Shapes applied everywhere, whether or not the meaning asks for them.

- **Automatic triplets.** Three adjectives, three nouns or three parallel examples by reflex: "clear, simple and effective". Keep three only when the meaning has three parts.
- **Repeated sentence openings.** Several sentences in a row starting with the same subject. Merge them or start with the action. People also repeat an opening on purpose for rhythm.
- **Symmetry.** Paragraphs all the same length, sentences all the same shape, balanced pairs one after another.
- **Synonym cycling.** "The company", "the firm", "the organization" for the same thing in one paragraph. If one word is right, repeat it.
- **Trailing participle.** A tail that pretends to explain: "highlighting the importance of", "reflecting a broader shift". Replace it with the fact or cut it.
- **"Not only... but also"** and **"Whether you're... or..."** used as all-purpose frames.
- **Em dash as the universal connector.** *Weak alone.* People use the em dash in English, so one proves nothing. The tell is several per paragraph, for every aside and every pause. Follow the person's own rate: if their hand-written texts have none, use commas, parentheses or a full stop.
- **Stacked qualifiers.** *Weak alone.* "It could potentially be argued that it might". Keep one qualifier when the doubt is real. Ordinary hedges such as "perhaps" or "tends to" are human habits.
- **Hyphen after the noun.** *Weak alone.* "The report is high-quality, the process is well-documented." The hyphen belongs before the noun ("a high-quality report").

## Inflation and borrowed authority

The fact underneath is usually sound. Keep it and remove the dressing.

- **Declared importance.** "Marks a turning point", "plays a key role", "stands as a testament to". State the fact and let the reader judge.
- **Avoiding "is" and "has".** "Serves as", "stands as", "functions as", "boasts", "features". Use the simple verb.
- **Vague connection.** "Associated with", "linked to", "tied to" without saying how. Name the relationship, or keep the vague wording if you do not know it.
- **Brochure language.** "Nestled in the heart of", "breathtaking", "rich heritage", "must-visit". Say what the thing is.
- **Borrowed authority.** "Experts agree", "studies show", "industry reports", or a list of famous outlets. Name the source and what it said, or drop the claim.
- **Instructions to the reader.** "This point is crucial", "This is where it gets interesting". If the point is clear they are not needed.
- **Inflated words.** Delve, leverage, utilize, foster, harness, elevate, unlock, robust, seamless, pivotal, crucial, intricate, tapestry, landscape, realm, showcase, underscore, testament, vibrant, journey (figurative), game changer. This list ages fastest: check it against current AI drafts.
- **Empty adverbs.** Simply, truly, fundamentally, crucially, inherently, incredibly, ultimately, quietly.

## Formatting by rule

Templates and editors also produce clean formatting. The tell is decoration on every item.

- **Bold as decoration.** Bold scattered mid-sentence, or every list item shaped as a bold label, a colon and an explanation.
- **Decorative headings.** Title Case On Every Heading, emoji or arrows in headings, a rule between every section.
- **A heading repeated in the first sentence.** "Performance" followed by "Speed matters." Start with the content.
- **Lists where two sentences would do**, and a heading over every two-line paragraph.
- **Curly quotes.** *Weak alone.* Curly marks in a chat message from someone whose other messages have straight ones. Most editors curl quotes automatically.

## Leftovers from the chat and the draft

Remove these outright.

- **Chatbot residue.** "Great question!", "Certainly!", "I hope this helps", "Let me know if you'd like me to expand". The most certain tell, and easy to miss when it wraps real content.
- **Assistant formulas in emails.** "I hope this email finds you well", "I wanted to reach out", "Please don't hesitate to reach out", "Thank you in advance for your time" on every message. Keep one only if the person really uses it.
- **Knowledge-limit disclaimers.** "As of my last update", "While specific details are limited", followed by a plausible guess. Say what is not known, or cut the sentence.
- **Writing about the document.** "The table below compares", "This section is organized by", "This was added to replace". Describe the subject, not the page.

## Writing for the wrong reader

- **Re-explaining what the reader knows.** A model writes for someone with no context. In a reply, the reader has the context: restating the problem and walking through the evidence buries the decision in the last line. Lead with the decision and keep only the one fact the reader lacks.

## When not to act

- Inside a quotation, a title or a proper name.
- Greetings and sign-offs on letters and emails: they are older than chatbots.
- Texts written before late 2022: they are not AI-written, whatever they look like.
- A single *weak alone* tell with nothing around it.
- Anything the person's own samples show they do.

## Two quick tests

- **The moving test.** If a sentence could move unchanged into another person's text, another company's or another subject's, it is filler. Cut it or replace it with a fact, an example, a consequence.
- **The read-aloud test.** If the sentence sounds like a speech when read to a colleague, rewrite it the way you would say it.
