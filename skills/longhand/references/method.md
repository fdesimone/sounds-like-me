# Analysis method

How to choose samples and what to look for. Read this before collecting texts. It applies to every language.

## Which samples to trust

Many people now write with AI help. A profile built on those texts is a portrait of the AI, not of the person. So samples have to be weighed.

Highest trust:

- an AI draft and the hand rewrite of the same text. It shows what the person cuts, adds and changes when a text does not sound like them.

High trust:

- texts written before the person used AI. They are certainly theirs: check the date. Anything from before late 2022, when chat assistants became common, is safe;
- short emails and messages they sent. Almost nobody asks AI to write a two-line reply;
- rules the person states. They beat the samples.

Doubtful:

- long, recent, very tidy texts. They may have been written or polished with AI: ask;
- hybrid documents (an AI draft touched up) without the original draft. AI residue remains and you cannot tell it from their voice.

Low trust:

- documents with several authors, forms, company templates. You do not know who wrote what.

Signs that a text was written with AI: it is much longer and more formal than the same person's other emails, it uses textbook courtesy formulas, it cites rules or references with unusual precision, it has no typos while the others do. When in doubt, ask the person or leave it out.

In email threads read only the part the person wrote, not the quoted messages below it.

### How much is enough

A good start: about ten short emails, two long texts, one draft and rewrite pair. Less still works, but the limit goes into the skill.

### The draft and rewrite pair

If the person does not have one, suggest making it: have an AI write a page on a subject they know and ask them to rewrite it the way they would. Ten minutes of work worth more than a hundred emails.

To compare the two versions fill in three lists:

- **What they cut.** Whole passages, sentence types, punctuation marks, formulas. Note how much shorter the text gets.
- **What they add.** Personal judgments, examples from their own trade, questions, qualifications.
- **What they change.** Grammatical person, tone, order of arguments, word choice.

Treat anything that survives unchanged with care: they may like it, or they may have missed it. Check whether the same pattern appears in texts written from scratch. If it never does, it is residue.

## What to look for

For each dimension note the trait and a short example.

**Structure.** Where does the point go: first, or after a preamble? How do they close: a request, a question, a greeting, nothing?

**Length.** How many sentences in a typical email? How long is a paragraph? When rewriting, do they shorten or expand?

**Grammatical person.** I, we, impersonal. Formal or informal address, and with whom.

**Stance.** Do they say what they think or stay neutral? How do they soften, stress, admit a limit?

**Vocabulary.** Recurring connectives, intensifiers, idioms, trade jargon, foreign words left untranslated, words they never use.

**Figures of speech.** Asides, everyday metaphors, questions, irony, lists, repetition, understatement. For each: how often and with what restraint.

**Rhythm.** Long or short sentences, coordinated or subordinated, how they alternate.

**Punctuation.** Favorite marks and absent ones. Keep habit apart from error.

**Formulas by register.** Greeting, opening, closing and signature, separately for each kind of recipient (people they know, offices, clients, superiors).

**Formatting.** Bold, lists, headings, tables, emoji.

**Moods.** How the writing changes when they are annoyed, grateful, in a hurry.

## What carries a voice

A model's default choices are the ones that fit everyone. A voice lives in the choices that fit only this writer. When you read the samples, look for these and make sure the profile protects them:

- **Specific, unusual details.** A real place, an odd comparison, the example only someone in their trade would pick.
- **Mixed feelings.** A judgment left partly unresolved: "mostly good, but something bothers me".
- **Asides and self-corrections.** A parenthesis that qualifies, a "or rather" in mid-sentence.
- **Dated or local references.** Slang, in-jokes, expressions tied to a place, a company or a period.
- **Choices they can explain.** A first-person decision with its reason.

These are the first things a polished rewrite removes, and the first things a reader who knows the person would miss.

Sentence and paragraph habits are more stable than favorite words. Weight structure, rhythm and formulas above vocabulary lists, which shift with time and subject.

## From material to profile

- Pick five to eight traits: the most frequent ones, the ones a reader would notice.
- Write each trait as a working instruction followed by the reason and an example.
- Split registers only when they really differ.
- In examples remove names, addresses, numbers, amounts and any reference to third parties. From work documents quote only short, generic fragments, and ask first whether the document is confidential.
- List separately what must not be imitated (typos, slips) and what you could not observe.

## Common mistakes

- **Describing the person with adjectives.** "Direct, professional tone" helps nobody. "The first sentence of the email already states the request" does.
- **Copying the whole list of patterns to avoid.** Keep the entries that matter, with their own examples.
- **Taking AI residue for style.** It happens with hybrid documents.
- **Making the profile better than the person.** See principle 2.
- **Forgetting formats without samples.** State them in the limits.
